for 15 min. Then a SnO2 layer was spin coated once again and
annealed in ambient air at 180 /C176C for 30 min. After that, 1.5 M
PbI2 solution (N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) : Dimethyl sulfoxide

Finally, carbon electrode was deposited on the perovskite ﬁlm by
doctor blade to obtain the HTM-free carbon-based PSCs devices.
All above steps were performed in ambient air (RH ~ 30%).
2.4. Measurements and characterization
The morphology of samples was characterized by a ﬁeld-
emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI Verios G4) and
a transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Talos F200X). The
distribution of doped Ni atom on graphene was investigated by a
high-angle annular dark-ﬁeld imaging-scanning transmission elec-
tron microscopy (HAADF-STEM, JEOL ARM-200F). The Raman spec-
trum of doped graphene was recorded using a confocal microscope
Raman spectrometer (Alpha 300R) with a TEM00 laser at 532 nm.
The elemental composition and elemental chemical state of sam-
ples was measured by an X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS,
Axis Supra) equipped with a monochromatic Al Ka X-ray source,
and the binding energy scale was adjusted assigning the signal of
C 1s to 284.8 eV. The work function of samples was obtained by
an Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) system integrated
into XPS with an excitation photon energy of 21.22 eV. The steady-
state photoluminescence (PL, excitation at 510 nm) spectrum was
acquired using a ﬂuorescence spectrometer (PicoQuant Fluo Time-
300). The current–voltage (J-V) characteristics of the PSCs were
measured using a Keithley 2420 source meter under the air mass
1.5 illumination at 100 mW cm/C02 (Newport 91,160 solar-light sim-
ulator). All J-V tests were performed under ambient conditions at
room temperature. Electrochemical impedance spectruscopy (EIS)
was recorded by electrochemical workstations (CHI 760E) with
applied bias from 0.75 V to 0.95 V and a frequency range of
10 MHz to 0.1 Hz under dark condition.
3. Results and discussion
Ni-NG shown in Fig. 1a is transparent and wrinkled on the sur-
face, which still exhibits a thin two-dimensional structure of gra-
phene carrier, with the advantages of large speciﬁc surface area
and excellent electrical conductivity [15,38]. The Raman spectrum
presented in Fig. 1b is the NG and Ni-NG with different Ni doping
concentration, which is prepared by adding different amounts of
nickel nitrate solution as nickel source through the impregnation
method. All samples have two typical Raman peaks of few-layer
graphene, a D band at around 1350 cm/C01 and a G band at around
1590 cm/C01, suggesting that the doping of Ni does not affect the
intrinsic structure of graphene [41]. In addition, the ratio of D
and G band can be used to characterize the defect degree of gra-
phene. When the Ni doping concentration increases from 1 /C2 10-
4 to 4 /C2 10-4 mol/g, the ratio of D and G band are 1.02, 1.04 and
1.06, respectively. Consequently, the incorporation of Ni into gra-
phene lattice increases the dislocation and vacancy of Ni-NG. How-
ever, the ratio of D and G band drops to 1.02 when Ni doping
concentration further increases to 8 /C2 10-4 mol/g, which indicates
that excessive Ni atoms can decrease defects in the structure of Ni-
NG due to the reduction of single atomic sites on graphene by
atomic agglomeration. EDS mapping of Ni-NG in Fig. 1c clearly
demonstrates that Ni and N elements are homogeneously dis-
tributed on graphene. In order to obtain more information about
the distribution of Ni atoms, HAADF-STEM of Ni-NG is recorded
in Fig. 1d. Isolated bright spots can be associated to Ni atoms, con-
ﬁrming that single-atom Ni sites exist on graphene without obvi-
ous agglomeration [42].
To further investigate the elemental composition and state of
Ni-NG, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is performed for
NG and Ni-NG with different Ni doping concentration. As seen in
Fig. 2a, all Ni-NGs appear a peak signal of Ni 2p, which demon-
strates the successful doping of Ni atoms in graphene. The element
contents of the above products are summarized in Table 1. With
the increase in nickel nitrate introduction during preparation, the
Ni content of Ni-NG can be obtained from 0.09% to 0.50%, indicat-
ing that by incorporating different amount of nickel nitrate as
nickel source can effectively regulate the Ni doping concentration.
The high-resolution N 1s spectrum is displayed in Fig. 2b. After Ni
doping, the peak shape of N 1s is changed from a broad single peak
to double peaks, which implies that the Ni atoms changes the
chemical state of N. In addition, With the Ni doping concentration
increasing, N 1s peak is shifted to the higher binding energy and
the Ni 2p peak is shifted to the lower binding energy (Fig. S1),
which suggests that electrons transfer from N atoms to Ni atoms
via Ni-N coordination. Fitted high-resolution N 1s spectrum of
NG in Fig. 2c reveals the presence of four types of N species with
pyridinic N (398.5 eV), pyrrolic N (401.2 eV), graphitic N
(403.5 eV) and N-O (405.6 eV) [43,44]. It reported that the domina-
tion of pyridinic N could improve the chemical activity of NG and
facilitate the anchoring of Ni atoms on NG via chemical bonds to
obtain more Ni active sites. Fitted high-resolution N 1s spectrum
of Ni-NG synthesized by 2 /C2 10-4 mol/g nickel nitrate introduction
in Fig. 2d evidences the presence of Ni-coordinated Ni-N moieties
located at the typical position around 399.8 eV [42].
The Ni-NG is introduced at the perovskite layer/carbon elec-
trode interface by spin-coating method to construct composite
electrodes. Fig. 3a-b present the top-view SEM images of
perovskite/Ni-NG, it can be clearly seen that the Ni-NG completely
covers the perovskite ﬁlm, which is beneﬁcial to the charge trans-
port because of good interface contact. When the Ni-NG dispersion
concentration increase from 1 mg/mL to 5 mg/mL, more large-
sized Ni-NG agglomerates appear on the surface of perovskite ﬁlm
(Fig. S2), which is due to that Ni-NG layers are easily stacked
together in high concentration dispersion. The presence of agglom-
erates not only decreases the contact area between Ni-NG and per-
ovskite, but also reduces the exposure of Ni active sites. The typical
cross-sectional SEM image of the PSCs based composite electrodes
is recorded in Fig. 3c. The PSCs device is a well-deﬁned layer-by-
layer structure without hole transport layer, mainly composed of
a composite electrode (30 lm), a MA0.8FA0.2PbI2.9Br0.1 layer
(470 nm), a SnO2 layer (60 nm), and a FTO glass. Particularly, a
large amount of carbon black particles with diameters
of ~ 10 nm tightly adhere to the surface of the Ni-NG and the com-
posite electrode is stably attached to the perovskite layer. The
schematic illustration of the hole extraction from perovskite by
Ni-NG is shown in Fig. 3d. Ni-NG with a high density of single-
atom Ni active sites can act as a fast channel for hole transport
and thus effectively improve charge transport.

electrode greatly affects the ability of hole extraction and trans-
