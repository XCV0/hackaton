the light-to-power conversion efﬁciency to absorber thickness
ratio,[10,13,14] and lower material consumption.[15] Such beneﬁts
align with various compliance requirements of regional legisla-
tion, for instance, with the European Commission’s guidelines
aimed at reducing the use of critical raw materials, being crucial
for the dissemination of building-integrated PV.[16–18]
Nonetheless, there is a signiﬁcant efﬁciency drop following
the pursuit of ultrathin conﬁgurations, mostly due to:
1) increased rear interface recombination and 2) incomplete light
absorption.[10,16,17,19–21] To address the rear interface recombina-
tion, passivation schemes based on patterned dielectric layers,
such as SiO2,[20,22–25] Al2O3,[26–30] HfO2,[31,32] and TiO2,[33,34]
have been efﬁciently introduced, yielding promising enhance-
ments in open-circuit voltage (VOC), with improvements over
100 mV compared with a nonpassivated device.[20] However, to
tackle the challenges imposed through the absorber thickness
reduction, the electrical gains provided by dielectric passivation
contact schemes need to be coupled with signiﬁcant optical
gains, as thinning down the absorber from /C252000 to 500 nm
leads to losses in the short-circuit current density ( JSC) that
can be as high as 4 mA cm/C02.[17] The dielectric rear interface pas-
sivation architectures already allow for a rear optical reﬂectance
improvement, usually around 0.1–0.9 mA cm/C02,[20,22,35,36] due to
a refractive index mismatch between the absorber and the dielec-
tric layer with a low refractive index.[19,36] Nevertheless, consid-
ering a CIGS-based absorber with a bandgap energy value of
1.16 eV and assuming an ideal charge carrier collection, even
if a perfect metallic mirror is integrated at the rear of an ultrathin
device with a 500 nm absorber, the simulated JSC value is still
2mAcm/C02 lower than the one of the conventional architecture
with a 2 μm absorber.[17] Hence, besides an increased optical rear
reﬂection with the passivation layer, incorporated scattering strat-
egies to further improve light absorption remain a crucial aspect
to attain high-performance ultrathin solar cells. Plasmonic nano-
particles (NPs) have emerged as a compelling solution, having
been integrated into several solar cell technologies.[37–40] The
NPs can signiﬁcantly enhance light interaction through plas-
monic resonance effects, which involve the collective oscillation
of free electrons within the NP when exposed to speciﬁc light
wavelengths, which may lead to both far-ﬁeld scattering and
near-ﬁeld localization, increasing the chances of photon absorp-
tion within the absorber.[21,41,42] Regarding the placement of
metallic NPs within an ultrathin CIGS-based solar cell architec-
ture, there are reports of these being placed at either the solar cell
front surface or at the p–n junction[43–45] or integrated within the
rear contact architecture.[46,47] However, if the NPs are placed
above the absorber, those would block a portion of the incident
light, through reﬂectance or parasitic absorbance.[17,21]
Therefore, a more beneﬁcial approach involves placing NPs at
the absorber rear.[17,21,48] In this conﬁguration, the NPs will inter-
act with long-wavelength light not absorbed during the initial tra-
versal through the solar cell, thereby minimizing undesirable
parasitic optical losses.[49] At resonant wavelength values, the
NPs may scatter or absorb the incoming light; thus, the absorbed
light in the metallic NPs will not lead to photocurrent generation.
Hence, the plasmonic architecture should be optimized for an
optical path length enhancement by maximizing the scattering
efﬁciency rather than the absorption one. However, the integra-
tion at the absorber rear requires the encapsulation of the metal-
lic NPs, as the CIGS-based layer deposition temperatures between
300 and 600 °C could lead to in-diffusion.[17,21,50–52] Thus, some
architectural solutions have been proposed, such as integration
within a transparent conductive oxide (TCO),[47] albeit without
the demonstration of solar cell devices, or even the use of ther-
mally stable Mo NPs.[46] Nevertheless, encapsulating the NPs with
a thick TCO dampens the scattering efﬁciency, and using Mo with
weak scattering resonances requires very large NPs that hamper
the absorber growth, leading to VOC value degradation.
Consequently, an architecture involving the encapsulation of
NPs with a thin dielectric rear passivation layer has been pro-
posed.[17,53,54] In a prior study, the effective encapsulation of
micrometer-sized Au NP aggregates with a 25 nm Al2O3 layer
in a complete CIGS-based device was demonstrated through
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy depth analysis.[54] The integration
of these metallic aggregates resulted in an optical enhancement.
However, the developed Au aggregates, being micrometer sized,
created a highly irregular and rough rear interface, which contrib-
uted to a decrease in the VOC and conversion efﬁciency values in
comparison to a reference device.[54] Thus, in this work, a combi-
nation of a controlled procedure using a microﬂuidic device to
deposit individualized 105 nm Au NPs with a nanoimprint lithog-
raphy (NIL) approach to create electrical contacts was developed.
Therefore, a substrate that combines passivation through the
dielectric scheme while allowing for the utilization of the substan-
tial passivation area (> 70%) for effective light trapping through
individual metallic NPs was achieved. The proposed architecture
allows to have NPs close to the solar cell absorber, effectively
exploiting the plasmonic scattering of small NPs that do not ham-
per the absorber layer growth and degrade the device performance.
The developed plasmonic and passivated architecture allowed for a
3.7 mA cm/C02 JSC increase over a nonpassivated reference device, as
well as a 23 mV VOC improvement. The combined passivation and
optical improvement from the novel architecture led to an almost
2% absolute light-to-power conversion efﬁciency enhancement in
comparison to a nonpassivated reference device. The attained
results show the potential to couple optical with electrical strategies
through the exploitation of plasmonic NPs encapsulated by a
dielectric passivation scheme in ultrathin solar cells.
