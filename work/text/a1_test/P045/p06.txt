as the removal of the PDMS device from the Al2O3 layer without
hampering its pristine properties, since the substrate/device
bonding was difﬁcult to break without leaving some PDMS waste
on the substrate, Figure 4b. Thus, modiﬁcations to the plasma
activation step were performed in the developed protocol, as it
has a signiﬁcant inﬂuence on the adhesion between substrate
and device.[68,78,79] Firstly, the deposition process was conducted
without the plasma treatment, Figure 4c, which resulted in a
nonuniform deposition with low surface coverage due to a weak
device bonding that led to the NPs solution leak. The O2 plasma
treatment was then applied only in the substrate, in order to
remove surface contaminants that may inhibit a good adhesion
without creating reactive chemical groups in the PDMS that
hinder the device removal.[78,79] Through this approach, a
homogeneous deposition was achieved, Figure 4d, and the inter-
face bond could be readily disintegrated without leaving any
residual PDMS on the substrate. To enhance the surface cover-
age, the deposition time was also increased from 2 to 12 h,
Figure 4e, achieving a NPs surface coverage of 10%. The opti-
mized procedure, regarding the O2 plasma treatment and
deposition time, was employed for subsequent deposition
processes.
2.4. Solar Cell Device Development
The developed Grown NPs solution was used to deposit the
105 nm NPs on the 1.1 mm SLG/350 nm Mo/10 nm Al2O3 sub-
strate, through the optimized microﬂuidic device deposition pro-
cedure. A top-view SEM image of the substrate after the Grown
NPs solution deposition is shown in Figure 5a. The attained NPs
surface coverage value for the deposition of the as-Grown solu-
tion was 3%, much lower than the surface coverage presented in
Figure 4e. Such discrepancy lies in the NPs concentration differ-
ence between the solutions used in each deposition process.
However, the 42 nm solution used in the deposition optimization
steps had a particle concentration of 1.74/C210/C010 M, the Grown
NPs solution has an estimated particle concentration of
8.7/C210/C012 M.[80] A lower concentration for the Grown NPs solu-
tion is a result of the two-step method to attain the large diame-
ter, as this process consists of the addition of the seed NPs to a
premixed solution of HAuCl4 and hydroxylamine-hydrochloride.
Since no new NPs are being formed, only seeds growth, there is a
drop in the particle concentration as the solvent volume is
increased.[67] To enhance surface coverage, the Grown NPs solu-
tion concentration was increased by a factor of 4 (4/C2 concen-
trated) and 8 (8/C2 concentrated) through the process of
centrifuging a speciﬁed volume of the solution and subsequently
redispersing the resulting precipitate into smaller volumes. The
4/C2 and 8/C2 concentrated solutions were used in the microﬂuidic
deposition procedure leading to increased surface coverage val-
ues of 6% and 7%, for the 4/C2 and 8/C2 concentrated solutions,
respectively, Figure 5b,c. The diffuse reﬂectance spectra from
the substrates with as-Grown, 4/C2, and 8/C2 concentrated NPs
are shown in Figure 5d. The substrate with the NPs deposited
from the as-Grown NPs solution shows the lowest diffuse reﬂec-
tance spectra out of the three substrates. By increasing the solu-
tion concentration 4/C2, there is an increase in the diffuse
reﬂectance values with the dipole resonance peak at 600 nm cor-
responding to a value of 3.1%, as a result of the surface coverage
enhancement. However, the substrate with the 8/C2 concentrated
solution has a slightly lower and broad resonant peak, despite
having the highest surface coverage. In this case, a substantial
rise in concentration through the centrifugation processes led
to the formation of large clusters (Figure 5c) and an uneven depo-
sition pattern. The nonuniform deposition was reﬂected in vary-
ing surface coverage values across different analyzed areas. Thus,
the decrease in the diffuse reﬂectance peak for the 8/C2 concen-
trated in comparison to the 4/C2 one is an indication that to ensure
an efﬁcient scattering substrate the surface coverage must be
accomplished through a uniform deposition with individualized
NPs. Nevertheless, even a few small aggregates are present in the
4/C2 concentrated substrate. In this process, sodium citrate was
used as the NPs stabilizing agent, providing an electrostatic
repulsion between NPs that counterbalances the attractive Van
der Waals forces.[71] Nonetheless, the weak electrostatic repul-
sion may fail under repeated centrifugation processes to increase
the solution concentration, forming NPs aggregates.[81]
