Regarding the voltage and collection efﬁciencies, there is a criti-
cal thickness of about 70 nm beyond which these parameters
tend to increase and saturate for a thickness of about 123 nm,
remaining almost constant beyond this value.
Finally, the VIM analysis was extended to study the series
resistance Rs of the devices. According to the equivalent circuit
of the device, the derivative dV/dJ as a function of 1/J, both in the
dark and under illumination,[25] forms a straight line, where
the slope is nkT/q (with n being the diode’s ideality factor)
and the intercept corresponds to Rs.
Figure 10 shows the results of this study. In the dark, the value
of Rs increases with thickness, reaching nearly 100 Ω cm2 for the
thickest device; see Figure 10a. However, under illumination, Rs
decreases drastically to a value of 4–6 Ω cm2, which is virtually
the same for all devices. This result suggests that the electrode
resistance (AZO þ FTO) is the limiting factor for Rs under illu-
minated conditions, while the effect of internal resistance,
though signiﬁcant in the dark, has little inﬂuence under
illumination.
The difference ΔRs between Rs and its saturation value at high
illumination can be interpreted as the internal resistance of the
absorber and 1/ΔRs, therefore, as the photoconductance of the
absorber. Figure 10b shows the dependence of 1/ΔRs on illumi-
nation. Assuming that Jsc approximates the photocurrent
generated optically in the absorber, it is possible to estimate
the μτ product associated with the photoconductivity. The values
obtained are in the range of 10/C08–10/C07 cm2 V/C01, which are very
similar to those found in a-Si:H using conventional techni-
ques.[29] In a sense, the discrepancy with the values obtained
from the VIM analysis highlights the limitations of the assump-
tions in the model used to interpret the measurements, particu-
larly regarding the real meaning of the effective μτ derived from
these measurements.
3.2.3. Spectral Response Measurements
Spectral response measurements are valuable to characterize the
photocurrent and are commonly used to determine the losses
responsible for reducing the measured Jsc from the maximum
achievable photocurrent. EQE is a dimensionless parameter
given by the number of electrons which exit the device per inci-
dent photon at each wavelength. The photocurrent is the integral
over wavelength of the product of the measured EQE with the
illumination spectrum, AM1.5G for terrestrial standard solar
cells.[30] The IQE for transparent solar devices was calculated
using Equation (7).
IQE ¼ EQE 1 /C0 R /C0 T (7)
As the absorber thickness increases, EQE initially improves
but then decreases or broadens, particularly around the 400 nm
peak. Thinner absorbers may not fully capture incident light,
while thicker absorbers face increased recombination losses.
Additionally, signiﬁcant reﬂection losses, particularly at the
450 nm peak, further reduce efﬁciency. Beyond 600 nm,
EQE declines across all samples, with this decrease correspond-
ing to the absorber bandgap, which limits the ability of the
material to convert lower energy photons into electron ﬂow
(Figure 11).

while internal losses can be well managed, further optimization
of the antireﬂection coating would increase both PCE and LUE
values. In any case, we can estimate that by reducing reﬂection
losses in the wavelength range 350–500 nm, values of LUE
