the “First solar-type” production and assembly line of CdTe PV modules, 
offering advantages with respect to material availability and “energy- 
payback” period [8]. While that stage is being awaited, chemical 
deposition route permits many research groups to get involved to solve 
many of the materials and interfacial problems of the cell structure, 
identified in it [8,9]. Modest experimental facility may be adequate to 
produce the CD-solar cells and hence make improvements in the win-
dow/material/interfacial components and in the cell parameters over 
those reported here. For example, a 2025 study on Sb2S3 solar cells re-
ported the use of a modified nanostructured SnO2 layer treated in an 
ammonium fluoride solution on which CdS thin film was deposited. This 
was followed by the deposition of the Sb2S3 absorber film, which was 
also modified with chemical additives moderating the reaction. Conse-
quently, its η increased to 8.26 % [23]. The introduction of LiF was 
found to improve the n-type character of the CdS thin films, and inhibit 
the inherent defects in the antimony sulfide selenide thin films produced 
by hydrothermal chemical deposition. Consequently, in 
FTO/CdS/Sb-S-Se solar cells, η rose to 10.76 % [24]. Similarly, the use of 
a “hole-selective interfacial layer” in the cell structure, 
FTO/CdS/Sb-S-Se/interfacial layer/contact, was found to take η to 9.13 
%, where the layer was related to a benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b’]dithiophene 
(BDT) analog [25].
The CD-cell route is well-suited for producing prototype PV modules 
of 3–4 % conversion efficiency [11,17] in school laboratories, perhaps 
by making use of better experimental facility available at a neighboring 
university laboratory for processing and characterization. Such pro-
totypes can help discuss in the class room their functionality in 
lighting-up white light emitting diodes or those of different colors, 
helping to appreciate Periodic Table of the Elements, from which the 
materials in the cell structure are constituted, and the interplay between 
“matter and energy at the quantum level” underlying the operation of 
PV and LED devices [26].
4. Perspectives on the variable bandgap antimony sulfide 
selenide solar cells
The approach made here to improve the open circuit voltage (VOC) of 
the solar cell by placing an absorber layer with a larger Eg near the 
window layer (CdS) and with a smaller Eg behind it to increase the JSC 
was derived from experimental data (Fig. 1) [5]. However, the need to 
increase the minority carrier concentration (electron, np) in the absorber 
layer also requires attention. For ideal homo-junction n+/p solar cells 
[19,20], at any operating temperature, VOC = constant ln (JL/Jo), where 
JL is the light generated current density (25–45 mA cm – 2) in the 
absorber layer (p-type) and the reverse saturation current density, Jo =
constant (ni 2/pp). Here, ni is the intrinsic carrier concentration in the 
absorber film, and pp is the majority carrier concentration (holes) in it. 
Thus, VOC is proportional to ln(pp/ni 2). While the use of an absorber of a 
larger Eg is one way of increasing VOC through a reduction in ni 2 this 
would reduce JL, and hence JSC and η of the solar cell [5]. Thus, an in-
crease in pp obtained through doping and/or guarding against the loss of 
S and Se in Sb2(SxSe1-x)3 during the thin film deposition and 
post-deposition processing is a necessity.
While doping methodology in the crystalline Si and III-V solar cells is 
well established, that is not so in commercial thin film CdTe solar cell 
technology [4]. Substitutional doping of Te-sites (Group-VI) with 
Group-V elements has been found to increase the VOC of CdTe solar cells 
[27]. Because antimony sulfide selenide thin film solar cells seen here 
essentially follow the scheme of commercial CdTe solar cells, they may 
benefit from the “lessons” learned from the CdTe solar cell technology 
[8]. So far, such doping technology has not been successfully integrated 
into Sb-S-Se solar cells [9]. With significantly distinct solubility products 
for substitutional dopant atoms in Sb-S-Se, doping may be difficult to 
achieve in the absorber films prepared by chemical deposition [28]. 
Doping may be easier to achieve in VT-cells through the addition of 
metal salts into the evaporation source mix [7]. Thermal processing in 
controlled ambient, but not essentially the fixed condition set for heat-
ing the cell stack at 280 ◦C for 30 min in nitrogen ambient at 20–30 torr 
reported here, may alter the cell performance. For example, a 2020 
study found that oxygen exposure of the back surface improved the 
performance of antimony sulfide selenide solar cells [29]. The avail-
ability of solar cells of η, 5–6 % prepared by CD and VTE methods re-
ported here may help adapt innovative approaches to improve these. 
The basic merit of antimony sulfide selenide solar cells is their opera-
tional stability [30], which has encouraged building prototype modules 
toward their commercialization [17,31].
5. Conclusions
In solar cells of antimony sulfide selenide absorber stacks of distinct 
bandgaps made by either chemical deposition (absorber thickness, 160 
nm) or by vacuum thermal evaporation (300 nm), solar radiation to 
electricity conversion efficiencies of above 5 % may be achieved. 
Improvement of the cell characteristics may require further work in 
achieving increased doping concentration in the absorber, the use back 
surface field layers to improve the collection efficacy for photo- 
generated carriers produced toward the back surface, and improved 
window layers to improve charge carrier collection.
