was improved by 33.1% and an efﬁciency of 9.37% was achieved.
Investigations show that K doping not only greatly improved the
crystallinity of the absorber but also achieved a very Cu-poor sur-
face with the K–In–Se phase formed on the absorber surface,
which accounts for the improvement of device performance.
2. Results and Discussion
To take advantage of the complexes as precursors, we ﬁrst tried to
synthesize complex GaTU3Cl3 but did not succeed because of the
hard Lewis acid nature of “Ga3þ” and soft Lewis base nature of
“TU.”[23] Then, we tried two strategies to prepare the aqueous
precursor solution. One was using CuTU3Cl, InTU3Cl3,
GaCl3, and thiourea as precursors and the other was using
CuCl, InCl3·4H2O, GaCl3, and thiourea as precursors. Our exper-
imental results demonstrated that either strategy is feasible for
preparing CIGS ﬁlms, while the devices based on the latter one
showed better efﬁciency and repeatability (see Figure S1,
Supporting Information). Therefore, here we focus on aqueous
solution using CuCl, InCl3·4H2O, GaCl3, and thiourea as precur-
sors. The molar ratio of Cu/(InþGa) was ﬁxed at 0.75, according
to our previous work,[19] with that of Ga/(InþGa) ﬁxed at 0.4.
According to our previous studies on the effect of K doping
on CIGS solar cells fabricated from DMF solution, the optimal
K/Cu molar ratio was found in the range of 0.010–0.020.[24] To
ﬁnd the optimal doping concentration, K/Cu molar ratios of
0.010, 0.015, and 0.020 were ﬁrst investigated and the results
demonstrated that 0.015 is the most appropriate one (see
Figure S2, Supporting Information). In the following studies,
the doping concentration of K has been ﬁxed at K/Cu molar ratio
of 0.015. The details of the precursor solution preparation and
device fabrication are given in the Experimental Section.
Figure 1 shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns and
Raman spectra of the precursor (P-CIGS) and absorber (CIGS)
ﬁlms without (CIGS w/o K) and with K (CIGS with
K) doping. For both precursor ﬁlms, only one broad diffraction
peak at around 28.00º that can be assigned to (112) diffraction of
CIS was observed, indicating the amorphous nature of the pre-
cursor ﬁlms, which is normally observed in solution-processed
precursor ﬁlms.[17] After selenization, sharp diffraction peaks
near 26.94/C14, 44.80/C14 and 53.13/C14, that can be indexed to the
(112), (220/204), and (312/116) diffractions of chalcopyrite
CuInSe2 (JCPDS#40–1487), are detected for absorber ﬁlms with-
out and with K doping, which indicates the formation of the
chalcopyrite-structured CIGS ﬁlm. The differences between
the two absorber ﬁlms are the slight shift of the diffraction angle
after K doping. From the enlarged (112) diffraction inserted, it
can be clearly seen that the (112) diffraction peak of the CIGS
ﬁlm without K doping locates at 26.94/C14 and shifts to 27.01/C14
for CIGS with K doping, which indicates the shrinking of the
lattice constant. The lattice constants of the CIGS without K
and CIGS with K are calculated from the XRD data and the
results are shown in Table 1. The smaller unit cell in Cu-poor
ﬁlms can be explained by more Se—VCu bonds, which are about
10% shorter than Se—Cu bonds.[25,26] In Figure 1b, the Raman
peaks for P-CIGS without K ﬁlm are observed at around 293 and
339 cm/C01, identiﬁed to the A1 mode and B2/E mode of
Cu(In,Ga)S2,[27] while for its selenized ﬁlm, these Raman peaks
are almost invisible and new peaks around 177 and 218 cm/C01
appear, which are identiﬁed to the A1 mode and B2/E mode
of Cu(In,Ga)Se2, respectively.[28] The same phenomenon
occurred with the K-doped precursor ﬁlm and its selenized ﬁlm.
No peaks of the Cu2/C0xSe secondary phase were detected.
