the grain boundaries at the front are strongly depleted in Ag, as
well as In- and Rb-enriched. Rubidium is also present at the
AIGS/CdS interface. This is true for the majority of the analyzed
areas/positions (see also Figure S11, supporting information, for
full cross-section), pointing to an (almost) complete Rb-coverage
of the AIGS surface after the PDT. This is in line with the XPEEM
results, showing Rb across the entire surface, even after H2O
rinse. To detect chemical changes across the ACIGS/CdS junc-
tion, an EDS line scan was extracted from the maps at the posi-
tion indicated as ‘LS1’ in the STEM image. The results are shown
in Figure 9c. Two notable features are detected. Firstly, a thin
(<10 nm) Rb-rich film is present between the AIGS chalcopyrite
absorber and the CdS buffer layer. This film mainly consists of In,
Se and Rb and maybe small amounts of Ga, but is fully depleted
in Ag. This strongly suggests the formation of a RbInSe2 layer
(maybe with traces of Ga) at the AIGS surface after RbF-PDT.
Alternatively, a Rb-In-Se layer with a stoichiometry different
from 1:1:2, such as 1:3:5 (RbIn3Se5) or 1:5:8 (RbIn5Se8), as ob-
served for OVCs, or even Rb-enriched In2Se3 may be thinkable.
However, at the position of the Rb peak, the stoichiometry
extracted from EDS already leads to a RbIn2.7Se5.7 compound.
Considering that the layer is very thin and no Rb plateau is
reached, it can be assumed that the real Rb content is signifi-
cantly higher in the film. Thus, we propose that a RbInSe2 film
is most likely.
Overlays of the STEM image with elemental maps of Cd, Ag, and
In or Rb are provided in Figure 10a,b,respectivelyandseparately

higher magnified, region of the heterojunction was analyzed,
which is shown in Figure S13, supporting information. From these
figures it becomes clear that the assumed RbInSe2 phase is not
always as ‘thick’ as at the position of ‘LS1’, but most often <5nm.
We believe that this corresponds to the porous surface structure
observed in SEM after water rinse (Figure 4). Still, it is surprising
that a RbInSe2 phase of several Nanometer thickness does not
result in a stronger Rb peak after the water rinse in XPS analysis,
while Rb is clearly visible in the corresponding XPEEM map.
Secondly, Cd seems to diffuse into the upper few nanometers of
the assumed RbInSe2 film, as suggested by the ≈5 nm shift of the
Cd onset as compared to the sulfur onset in Figure 9c. Both fea-
tures, the formation of a RbInSe2 layer and the Cd in-diffusion,
are perfectly in line with earlier observations obtained from
atom-probe tomography on low-gap CIGS absorbers subjected
to RbF-PDT (even the minor Ga incorporation into RbInSe2)[87].
Another EDS line scan was extracted across a grain boundary at
position ‘LS2’ (indicated in the STEM image). The results in
Figure 9d confirm the Ag-depletion and In- and Rb-enrichment
in the grain boundary, while Se and Ga do not change.
Overall, our results suggest that even when Cu is entirely
replaced by Ag in the chalcopyrite absorber, as it is the case
in this study, a very thin RbInSe2 surface layer forms upon
RbF-PDT. All other RbF-PDT-induced features, like the grain
boundary chemistry and the overall Rb distribution are also sim-
ilar for CIGS and AIGS solar cells.
Coming back to the solar cell performances presented and dis-
cussed in part 2.1, it may be speculated that the continuously
reduced FF with increasing RbF dose arises from a thicker or
more covering RbInSe2 layer. The CBO at the RbInSe2/AIGS
interface is expected to be much larger as compared to the
RbInSe2/CIGS interface [8, 39, 40, 42, 72], potentially explaining
why an optimized RbF-PDT can result in FF improvements for
CIGS, while the FF only deteriorates for AIGS. In addition, we
propose that the presence of excessive Rb at the back contact is
more harmful when a TBC is used, presumably by modifying the
GaOx properties and catalyzing its growth (i.e., making it
thicker), explaining why those samples show a larger FF loss
than the ones with Mo.
However, the question remains why no beneficial effect on VOC is
observed by the RbF treatment of AIGS absorbers, in contrast to
low- and wide-gap CIGS solar cells, as long as sufficient indium is
present. The only difference in this work is that Cu is completely
absent, indicating its crucial role for a VOC-boost after RbF-PDT.
A possible reason may be that the RbF-PDT did not lead to an
increase in doping density, as reported for CIGS [23–25]. All sam-
ples show more or less complete depletion (a typical feature of
high-Ag ACIGS absorbers) independent of the treatment, as
exemplified for 0 and 20 nm RbF-PDT in Figure S2 in the sup-
porting information. The physical origin may lie in the much
larger bulk solubility of Na and Rb for AIGS as compared to
CIGS absorbers, which determines the final concentration of
group-I vacancies [91].
3 | Conclusions
The effect of an RbF–PDT on the performance of Cu-free, wide-
gap (EG = 1.5 eV) AIGS solar cells with either opaque Mo or
transparent In2O3:W back contacts is studied. We find that the
FF monotonously deteriorates with increasing RbF dose for all
samples without observing any gain in VOC or JSC. The FF loss
is much more pronounced when In2O3:W is used, probably due
to a more detrimental effect of the presence of Rb at the AIGS/
GaOx/IOW back interface, where it replaces sodium. Rubidium
may even diffuse into the GaOx and negatively impact its electri-
cal properties. A facilitated GaOx formation by the RbF-PDT is
found, too. However, even for the maximum RbF dose, the
GaOx thickness only increases from ≈1.7 nm (no PDT) to
≈3.0 nm (20 nm RbF), which is deemed unlikely to explain
