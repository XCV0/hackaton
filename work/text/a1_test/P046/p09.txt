the in-house XPS and synchrotron-based XPEEM measurements
make a direct comparison of the emitted photoelectron spectra
challenging.
Overall, we suggest that the RbF-PDT results in the formation of
water-soluble Rb-, F-, and Ga-containing particles/agglomerates
at the surface. After water rinse (or CBD), those particles are gone
and only a very thin Rb-containing phase is left on the surface,
which is probably depleted in Ga. The SEM image in Figure 4
suggests that this phase either has local openings or varies lat-
erally in thickness. It should be mentioned here that the Rb3d
XPS-signal is very weak after the water rinse, but the correspond-
ing XPEEM measurement (presented in Figure S8 in supporting
information), being more surface-sensitive and conducted under
higher excitation flux, clearly shows a Rb signal across the entire
surface (albeit with significantly reduced intensity as compared
to before the water rinse). In line with the XPS analysis, the Ga-
rich particles (leading to Ga-In anti-correlation before water
rinse) vanished in the XPEEM maps after the water rinse.
2.2.2 | Chemical and Structural Modifications
underneath the Absorber Surface
Figure 7a,b shows the scanning transmission electron micros-
copy (STEM) cross-section images of the samples with standard
CdS and TBC without and with 20 nm RbF-PDT, respectively.
The dark spots are Ag precipitations, forming during lamella
preparation, as reported before for ACIGS solar cells [35, 44].
No significant structural changes are observed on the micrometer
scale after RbF-PDT. An open question raised in Section 2.1 was
whether the RbF-PDT leads to a thicker GaOx film at the AIGS/
IOW interface, potentially explaining the stronger FF deteriora-
tion for cells with IOW as compared to Mo. Indeed, as can be seen
in Figure 7c,d, which highly magnifies the back contact region, a
thicker GaOx layer (3.0 nm on average) is found after RbF-PDT as
compared to the sample without PDT (1.7 nm on average). The
latter value is in good agreement with our earlier work on ACIGS
with an IOW TBC [49]. However, a GaOx thickness of 3.0 nm is
still thinner as observed in other studies on (A)CIGS devices
with a TBC, which show no significant transport barrier and
high FF values for GaOx thicknesses up to at least 4 nm
[76–79]. Thus, it is deemed unlikely that the rather moderate
increase in GaOx extension from 1.7 to 3.0 nm alone explains
the strong photocurrent blocking in JV for the RbF-treated sam-
ples in this work.
Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) in STEM mode was
performed at the back contact region of the sample without RbF,
see Figure S9 in supporting information. Besides confirming the
presence of the GaOx layer, the analysis shows that the grain
boundaries close to the back contact are Ag-depleted and In-
enriched (same is true for majority of analyzed grain boundaries,
independent of their location, not shown here). A depletion of
group-I elements (Ag or Cu) is frequently observed in ACIGS
random, high-angle grain boundaries [18, 35, 87, 88] and claimed
to make them electrically more benign [89]. It was found that
with increasing Ga and for GGI > 0.4, grain boundaries in
CIGS are more likely to be group-I enriched and thereby poten-
tially more detrimental. However, no such Ag-rich grain bound-
aries were identified in this study.
The same STEM-EDS analysis was conducted for the correspond-
ing sample with 20 nm RbF-PDT, as illustrated in Figure 8a,b.
Again, the grain boundaries are Ag-depleted and In-enriched,
but in this case also decorated by rubidium. It was suggested that
the introduction of Rb leads to a slightly more pronounced (or fre-
quent) group-I depletion in CIGS grain boundaries [18, 87, 88],
which cannot be confirmed nor excluded in the present study.
We further find that Rb is also located at the AIGS/GaOx/IOW
interface of our samples. Extracted absolute and normalized
([0,1]) EDS line-scans across the back interface, presented in
Figure 8c,d, suggest that Rb may even be present inside the very
thin GaOx layer, potentially modifying its properties. Even the for-
mation of a Rb-Ga-O compound, such as RbGa5O11, may be think-
able [90].
In any case, it can be speculated that the excessive presence of Rb
at the back interface alters the electronic properties of one or sev-
eral of the involved layers (e.g., doping and band alignment) and
creates a back-contact barrier, explaining the strong FF losses
with increasing RbF dose. Even for CIGS solar cells with Mo back
contacts, the excessive replacement of Na by Rb atoms was sug-
gested to create a back-contact barrier [26, 73]. Indeed, we also
observe such an ion-exchange mechanism, resulting in a decreas-
ing Na content in the absorber bulk and at its interfaces with
increasing RbF dose (see alkali depth profiles in Figure S10, sup-
porting information). We conclude that an excessive concentra-
tion of Rb at the back-contact interface may be even more
detrimental when IOW is used instead of Mo.
From the EDS maps and the STEM image in Figure 8, it seems as
if GaOx locally grows spherically into the absorber. However, a
closer look reveals that those bright spots in the STEM image are
small voids at the interface, with GaOx at their edges and presum-
ably form when the NaF precursor layer was locally too thick, as
occasionally observed before when using a TBC [76, 77]. More
statistics are needed to determine whether such voids are more
frequent when a RbF-PDT is applied.
