transitions  [29−33].  In  particular,  the  oxygen  diffusion  at  the
SnO2/perovskite interface has been identified as a major contributor
to  the  instability  of  PSCs  [34−37].  In  2022,  Sui  et  al.  examined  the
role  of  SnO2 in  the  thermal  degradation  pathway  of  the  perovskite
layer  [38].  Their  findings  revealed  that  perovskite  materials  at  the
SnO2/perovskite  interface  are  prone  to  thermal  decomposition,
primarily due to the diffusion of oxygen atoms from the SnO2 layer
to the perovskite layer, facilitated by the low segregation energy for
oxygen  diffusion.  Building  on  this,  Park  et  al.  proposed  that  the
absence of oxygen atoms on the surface of SnO2 reduces the energy
barrier  for  the  formation  of  iodine  interstitials  (Ii),  which  would
accelerate  the  adverse  phase  transition  of  FAPbI3 [39].  To  mitigate
this,  they  used  black  phosphorus  quantum  dots  to  suppress  the
formation of oxygen vacancy (VO) in SnO2 and Ii in the perovskite,
leading  to  highly  stable  α-FAPbI3 crystal  films.  Additionally,  in
2022,  Zhong  et  al.  reported  the  use  of  potassium
chlorobenzenesulfonate  to  modify  the  SnO2/perovskite  interface  in
n–i–p  type  PSCs  [40].  The  C–Cl  bond  in  potassium
trichlorobenzene  sulfonate  could  effectively  interact  with
uncoordinated Sn and passivate the oxygen vacancies on the surface
of SnO2. By adjusting these vacancies, the thermal stability of PSCs
can be greatly improved. These findings underscore the importance
of  developing  effective  strategies  at  SnO2/perovskite  interface  to
inhibit  the  generation  of  oxygen  vacancies  in  SnO2 and  anchor
active  ions  at  their  sites  in  perovskite,  thereby  achieving  high-
stability PSCs.
Metal–organic  frameworks  (MOFs)  are  a  class  of  porous
skeleton  materials  composed  of  metal  node  units  and  organic
ligands,  which  possess  highly  spatially  confined  structures  and
uniformly  distributed  active  sites  [41−43].  This  unique  structural
feature facilitates the shielding of moisture and oxygen, thus greatly
enhancing the stability of devices [44, 45]. The surface active sites in
MOFs  can  effectively  passivate  defects,  stabilize  various  ions,  and
inhibit  ion  migration  [46−48].  In  2020,  Jen  et  al.  utilized  thiolated
two dimensional conjugated MOF (ZrL3) as electron transport layer
(ETL)  in  n–i–p  PSC,  where  the  MOF  effectively  trapped  free  Pb2+
ions,  achieving  a  PCE  of  22%  and  long-term  stability  for  1000  h
[49]. In 2021, Chen et al. introduced an ultra-thin europium-MOF
(Eu-MOF)  layer  at  the  SnO2/perovskite  interface  [50].  They  found
that  the  Eu-MOF  layer  could  increase  the  light  utilization  through
down  conversion  and  modify  perovskite  growth  conditions,
converting  residual  tensile  strains  into  compressive  strains.  More
recently,  in  2023,  Chen  et  al.  utilized  the  porous  nanostructure  of
zeolitic  imidazolate  framework-8  (ZIF-8)  to  form  a  porous  PbI2
film, promoting further crystallization of perovskite and achieving a
champion PCE of 24.08% with 90% residual efficiency after 1500 h
of  storage.  These  studies  highlight  the  potential  of  MOFs  in
enhancing both the efficiency and stability of PSCs. However, there
remains a significant gap in understanding the relationship between
MOF  structure  and  PSC  performance,  as  well  as  the  underlying
mechanisms,  particularly  the  influence  of  MOFs  on  oxygen
migration at the SnO2/perovskite interface.
In this work, we introduce a new functionalized MOF, UiO-66-
NH2-SH,  composed  of  zirconium  (Zr)  clusters  and
amino/sulfhydryl  group-modified  ligands.  This  MOF  is  employed
as  a  cross-layer  additive  between  the  SnO2 ETL  and  the  perovskite
layer.  The  porous  organic  framework  provides  anchor  points  that
promote  strong  bonding  between  the  perovskite  and  SnO2 layers,
while  its  functional –SH  and –NH2 groups  on  UiO-66-NH2-SH
regulate  perovskite  crystallization  and  passivate  interface  defects,
thereby  reducing  the  migration  pathways  for  oxygen  and  other
ions.  As  anticipated,  our  results  demonstrate  that  incorporating
UiO-66-NH2-SH  into  the  upper  layer  of  SnO2 effectively  hinders
oxygen  migration  from  SnO2 to  the  perovskite,  while  also
enhancing the perovskite grain size. Furthermore, UiO-66-NH2-SH
functions  synergistically  as  a  stable  unit,  a  behavior  not  observed
with  either  UiO-66  or  organic  molecules  with  similar  functional
groups.  As  a  result,  the  lifetimes  of  perovskite  devices  at  elevated
temperatures have been greatly extended. After storage at 85 °C in a
N2-filled  glove  box  for  2000  h  without  encapsulation,  the  MOF-
modified  device  maintained  over  100%  of  its  initial  efficiency,  in
stark  contrast  to  the  control  devices,  which  retained  only  about
40%. 
2    Results and discussion
In this work, the UiO-66-NH2-SH (Fig. 1(a)) was synthesized via a
solvothermal  method  [51],  incorporating  Zr6 nodes  and
multivariant  organic  linkers  functionalized  with –SH  and –NH2
groups. Through the coordination of –COOH with Zr, the specific
structure of UiO-66-NH2-SH is formed. The functional groups –SH
and –NH2 contained  in  the  organic  connectors  can  interact  with
SnO2 and  perovskite,  respectively,  strengthen  the  connection
between  SnO2 and  perovskite,  and  passivate  the  interface  defects.
Due  to  the  special  framework  structure  of  UiO-66-NH2-SH,  there
must  be “residual” functional  groups,  which  can  continuously
capture  escaping  O  elements  and  inhibit  O  migration  in  the
pathway of O migration, thus greatly improving the stability of the
devices.  As  shown  in Fig.  1(b),  the  powder  X-ray  diffraction
(PXRD)  pattern  of  UiO-66-NH2-SH  aligns  well  with  that  of  UiO-
66, indicating that the synthesized material retains the isostructural
crystal  architecture  of  the  UiO-66  framework,  thereby  confirming
the  successful  construction  of  UiO-66-NH2-SH.  Energy  dispersive
spectroscopy  (EDS)  mapping  (Fig.  1(c))  illustrates  that  the
distributions  of  nitrogen  (N),  Zr,  and  sulfur  (S)  are  uniformly
dispersed, confirming that the organic linkers are evenly integrated
within the UiO-66-NH2-SH structure.
MOFs with different ratios of –NH2 to –SH have been prepared
and  the –NH2/–SH  ratio  was  optimized  according  to  the
photovoltaic performance of their responding devices. As shown in
Fig.  S1  in  the  Electronic  Supplementary  Material  (ESM),  the
optimal preparation ratio of –NH2 to –SH is 1:5. Besides, the exact
–NH2/–SH ratio of the optimal MOF has been demonstrated to be
1:5.07 by the elemental analyzer (EA) testing, as shown in Table S1
in the ESM.
To  explore  the  influence  of  MOF  on  the  conductivity  of  SnO2
ETL,  we  conducted  conductivity  measurements.  As  shown  in  Fig.
S2  in  the  ESM,  the  introduction  of  UiO-66-NH2-SH  leads  to  a
significant  increase  in  conductivity,  likely  due  to  the  passivation  of
surface  defects  in  SnO2.  This  enhancement  is  favorable  for
improving the charge transport efficiency in PSCs.
Furthermore,  the  light  transmission  properties  of  SnO2 and
SnO2/MOF films were compared. As shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM,
both  samples  present  high  transmittance,  although  the
transmittance of SnO2/MOF film is slightly lower (~ 1%) than that
of  SnO2 film.  Thus,  we  speculate  that  the  impact  of  UiO-66-NH2-
SH on the light absorption of perovskite layer can be negligible.
