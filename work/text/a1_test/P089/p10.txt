From chemical analyses of the CIGS/buffer interfaces by
STEM/EDXS as depicted in Fig. S7 in the supplementary material,
a decreasing amount of Cu from In2S3 to In2O3 is detected within
the buffer, hinting at Cu diffusion from the CIGS absorber into the
buffer. We also found such a Cu enrichment in sputtered In2S3
buffers at Tsub = 220 °C with intentional Na incorporation by
time-of-flight secondary mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) depth pro-
files.34 Presumably, the affinity of copper to bond with S atoms is
high, and therefore, a correlation seems to exist between the amount
of Cu and the particular S content of the In2(OxS1−x)3 layer. For this
reason, the Cu content is highest for In2S3 [Fig. S7(b) in the
supplementary material] and appears to decrease with increasing O
content of the buffer layers [cf. Figs. S7(c)–S7(f) in the
supplementary material]. We observed such a pronounced Cu deple-
tion in the CIGS surface region as depicted in Fig. S7(b) in the
supplementary material for sputtered In2S3 at Tsub = 220 °C also on
similar samples with ToF-SIMS,16 which indicates the possible for-
mation of an ordered vacancy compound.

The formation enthalpy is computed from DFT energy differ-
ences at 0 K and zero pressure; thus, it equals the static inner
energy of the system. For pure In2S3, the formation enthalpy in the
spinel structure is considerably lower than in the bixbyite structure,
namely, 2.13 eV per anion. This huge energy difference indicates
that the In2S3 compound cannot crystallize in the bixbyite struc-
ture. For pure In2O3, the formation energy in the bixbyite structure
is 0.67 eV per anion lower than in the spinel structure, which indi-
cates that In2O3 will readily crystallize in the bixbyite structure. We
see from Fig. 7 that for an oxygen concentration of 75% or less, the
spinel structure is energetically more favorable than the bixbyite
structure. However, for higher concentrations of oxygen, the bixby-
ite structure is lower in energy. It is noteworthy that all mixed
In2(OxS1−x)3 configurations are still very high in energy, suggesting
a huge miscibility gap between In2S3 and In2O3. For comparison,
