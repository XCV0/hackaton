pure In2S3 as a buffer layer with thicknesses well above 20 nm
could lead to even higher absorption loss at the low wavelength
region and consequently a reduced blue response, resulting in a
limited short-circuit current density (JSC) compared to the cells
buffered with CdS. This scenario puts the original motivation, an
application of In2S3 as alternative buffer layer, into question and
urges engineering of the optical bandgap of In2S3 through extrinsic
doping or alloying. With sputtering from a suitably composed
target, it would be possible to realize high deposition rates and
reduce waste. Additionally, the fast and dry deposition process
from the gas phase would be better suited for an industrial environ-
ment. Moreover, it would have the advantage of better process inte-
gration for an in-line CIGS or even roll-to-roll module fabrication.
So far, CIGS-based 30 × 30 cm2 sub-modules reached efficien-
cies up to 17.9% with an evaporated InxSy:Na buffer.4 The good
efficiency can be elucidated in view of the facts that (1) intentional
incorporation of Na into the indium sulfide increases the bandgap
and (2) this InxSy:Na buffer modifies the band alignment at the
absorber/buffer interface and forms a favorable conduction band
offset.5–9 Another option to widen the bandgap of In2S3 is to sub-
stitute a part of the sulfur sites with oxygen. The study of optical
properties of oxygen-doped indium sulfide showed that depending
on the oxygen concentration, the bandgap can be effectively tai-
lored between 2.0 and 2.9 eV.10 Furthermore, the physico-chemical
characterization of the films studied by x-ray diffraction revealed
that the degree of crystallization drops substantially with increasing
oxygen content in the films. Niemi et al. introduced oxygen into
the In2S3 buffer by reactive sputtering and compared the change in
the fill factor (FF) of the cells with various concentrations of
oxygen.11 According to their report, the FF of the cells increased by
increasing the oxygen content in the sputter gas up to 1 vol. %, and
decreased again for higher concentrations of oxygen in In2S3.
However, the origin of the beneficial effect of low oxygen contents
in In2S3 on CIGS device performance is not clarified yet. Besides
the evaporation and sputtering of In2S3 implemented in an indus-
trial environment as described above, In2S3-based buffers were
grown by chemical bath deposition, atomic layer deposition,
various kinds of spray pyrolysis, ion layer gas reaction, metal
organic chemical vapor deposition, and electrodeposition and were
successfully adapted to CIGS-based thin-film solar cells (see
Mughal et al. and references therein).12
Note that widening the bandgap is not the single important
factor for an appropriate buffer layer. Since the main role of buffer
layers is to improve the quality of the pn-junction between the
absorber and transparent front contact, achieving a beneficial band
alignment at the buffer/absorber interface is a critical feature for a
suitable buffer layer. Therefore, a fundamental understanding of
the miscibility of oxygen in indium sulfide, crystal structure of the
mixed compounds, optical bandgap energy, the change in band
alignment at the buffer/absorber interface, and finally the suitability
of indium oxysulfide [In2(OxS1−x)3] compounds as buffer layers is
required.
An open question, in this context, is whether or not substitu-
tion on the anionic site would result in improved performance of
CIGS solar cells. In this contribution, we focus on physical and
chemical properties of magnetron-sputtered In2(OxS1−x)3 com-
pounds to shed more light on the viability of this material as an
alternative buffer system to the more commonly used solution-
grown CdS or Zn(O,S) buffer layers in CIGS thin-film solar cells.
II. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
A. Sample fabrication
1. CIGS thin-film solar cell preparation
CIGS thin-film solar cells were prepared with an in-line multi-
stage co-evaporation process on Mo-coated soda-lime glass.13
The CIGS layers had a thickness of around 2.3 μm, the
[Ga]/([Ga] + [In]) ratio was around 0.3, and the [Cu]/([Ga] + [In])
ratio was around 0.8 as determined by x-ray fluorescence measure-
ments. CIGS layers were doped with Na and partially K, which were
provided from the soda-lime glass substrate during CIGS deposition
at elevated deposition temperatures with substrate heater tempera-
tures around 450 °C (1st stage) and around 650 °C (2nd/3rd stage).
No additional alkali post-deposition treatment14 was applied after
the CIGS process. Subsequently, rf-sputtered In2(O,S)3 buffers with
different oxygen contents, rf-sputtered i-ZnO as the high-resistive
layer, and dc-sputtered ZnO:Al as the front contact were deposited.
The solar cells were completed with Ni/Al/Ni grid fingers, the total
cell area was 0.5 cm2, and no anti-reflective coating was applied.
2. Sputtering of In2(O,S)3 layers
The In2(OxS1−x)3 buffer layers were rf magnetron-sputtered in
a VON ARDENNE (Dresden, Germany) high-vacuum sputtering
system of type CS 730S from commercially available targets with a
diameter of 20 cm.15,16 The depositions were performed from five
different ceramic targets with nominal chemical compositions of
In2S3,In2(O0.25S0.75)3,In2(O0.5S0.5)3,In2(O0.75S0.25)3, and In2O3 at
power densities of 1 W/cm2. The substrate temperature Tsub ranged
from room temperature (RT) to 240 °C. In this work, we focus on
results achieved at room temperature, Tsub = 200 and 220 °C. As a
consequence of the target compositions, the oxygen in the layers
was added by two ways, either by reactive sputtering from a pure
In2S3 target by using different oxygen fractions in the Ar sputtering
gas in the range of 0–5 vol. % or by non-reactive sputtering from
the five different In2(OxS1−x)3 targets without any additional
oxygen in the gas flow (as described above). For this series with the
five ceramic targets, only for the pure In2O3 layers with thicknesses
above 300 nm (for optical and structural characterization), oxygen
was added to the gas flow to minimize occurrence of intrinsic
defects like oxygen vacancies. Two different pure In2S3 targets were
used, one for the sample series with reactive sputtering and the
other one for the series with the ceramic targets to avoid oxygen
contamination due to the gas flow. The layer compositions were
determined by energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDXS). In
case of non-reactive sputtering, the deposited In2(OxS1−x)3 layers
correspond to the nominal target compositions with x = 0, 0.25,
0.5, 0.75, and 1. A slight deviation can be observed by sputtering at
elevated temperatures.
For application as a buffer layer, the thickness of the
In2(OxS1−x)3 layers ranged from 30 to 90 nm. For characterization
by means of optical transmittance and x-ray diffraction (XRD)
measurements, thicker layers of 300–500 nm were grown on quartz
glass substrates.
