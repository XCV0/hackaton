The effects of TPFB on surface morphologies of perovskite
ﬁlms were investigated by scanning electron microscopy
(SEM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and conductive atomic
force microscopy (C-AFM) measurements. Figure 2A,D shows
top-view SEM images of pristine and TPFB-modiﬁed perovskite
ﬁlms, respectively. Both pristine and TPFB-modiﬁed perovskite
layers showed superior ﬁlm morphologies. Compared to pristine
MAPbI3 ﬁlm, the average grain size of TPFB-modiﬁed ﬁlm
enlarged from 500 nm to about 1 μm without deteriorating the
ultradense and uniform perovskite ﬁlm morphology, which
would be beneﬁcial for decreasing the nonradiative recombina-
tion.[24] The statistical distribution of the grain size is plotted in
Figure S5, Supporting Information. The cross-sectional SEM
images in Figure S6, Supporting Information, showed enlarged
and more vertical arrangements of perovskite grains. However,
the thickness demonstrated little to no variation. The achieved
superior perovskite grains were attributed to that TPFB could
retard the crystallization and growth rate of perovskite grain to
yield superior ﬁlm quality. The corresponding AFM images in
Figure 2B,E revealed variations in surface roughness.
Compared to pristine ﬁlm, the root-mean-square (RMS) rough-
ness of TPFB-modiﬁed ﬁlms increased from 8.2 to 13.6 nm. Note
that the slight increase in RMS might be helpful to light
trapping.[25] The 3D AFM image and related high distribution
in Figure S7, Supporting Information, also conﬁrmed the
increased roughness.
The spatially resolved electrical distributions of perovskite
ﬁlms were obtained by C-AFM measurements. As shown in
Figure 2C,F, the TPFB-modiﬁed perovskite ﬁlm demonstrated
a signiﬁcantly enhanced current signal when compared to the
pristine sample. Therefore, the incorporation of TPFB could
raise the carrier conduction. The difference in current signals
was further interpreted according to Figure S8, Supporting
Information, through line-proﬁle of C-AFM results.[26]
