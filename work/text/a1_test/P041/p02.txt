layer to prevent penetration of moisture.[17] The synergistic
effects induced by the effective passivation and hydrophobicity
of TPFB extremely enhanced the efﬁciency and stability of fabri-
cated PSCs devices. A maximum efﬁciency of 20.41% coupled
with ultrahigh Voc of 1.15 V was achieved with MAPbI3-based
PSCs. Besides, the superior passivation and hydrophobicity prop-
erty of TPFB allowed unencapsulated PSCs devices to maintain
85% of the initial efﬁciency after storage in the ambient environ-
ment for 1000 h. Furthermore, a PCE of 23.38% under reverse
scan (steady-state PCE of 23.2%) was achieved with double-cation
PSCs by introducing TPFB through a simple posttreatment pro-
cess. The negligible hysteresis (0.04) also convinced the superi-
ority of the TPFB modiﬁcation.
2. Results and Discussion
TPFB was dissolved in CB with various concentrations and intro-
duced into the perovskite ﬁlm through the antisolvent followed
by annealing process to fabricate the perovskite ﬁlm. To investi-
gate the interaction between TPFB and MAI, Fourier transform
infrared (FTIR) spectra of pristine and TPFB-modiﬁed MAPbI3
are gathered in Figure 1B, and full FTIR spectra are provided in
Figure S1, Supporting Information. Typical N─H vibrations
were noticed at around 3100 cm/C01. After TPFB modiﬁcation,
the N–H stretching vibrations shifted to lower wavenumbers,
indicating the formation of N–H…F hydrogen bonding.[18] As
the F element demonstrated the strong electronegativity, it would
attract the electron, thus weakened the N─H chemical bond and
decreased the corresponding the vibrational frequency. The large
steric hindrance inhibited the interaction of MAI and PbI2, slow-
ing down the crystallization process.[19]
Optical photographs of perovskite ﬁlms during the annealing
and crystallization processes are displayed in Figure S2,
Supporting Information. As can be seen, retarded crystallization
process took place. The uncontrolled crystallization induced
unwanted defects sites, which could serve as nonradiative recom-
bination centers.[20] The retarded crystallization rate caused by
the formation of hydrogen bonding could render high-quality
perovskite ﬁlms. According to the Ostwald ripening theory,
the incorporation of TPFB could enhance the Gibbs energy
through the formation of hydrogen bond, thereby decreasing
the nucleation sites and retarding crystallization to achieve
high-quality perovskite ﬁlms.[21] The formation of hydrogen bond
could enhance the activation energy, thus slowing down the reac-
tion between MAI and PbI2 to reduce the number of nuclei,
resulting in the retarded crystallization and larger grain size.
X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were carried out on the
resulting perovskite ﬁlms to further conﬁrm the enhanced crys-
tallization intensity. As shown in Figure 1C, the diffraction peak
of PbI2 at 12.6° was absent in both pristine and TPFB-modiﬁed
perovskite ﬁlm. By comparison, the (110) diffraction peak of
TPFB-modiﬁed ﬁlm at 13.9° signiﬁcantly increased, suggesting
the preferred orientation of the perovskite ﬁlm along the vertical
direction.[22] The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of (110)
diffraction peak was calculated and the data suggested a decline
in FWHM from 0.224 to 0.165, further verifying the enhanced
crystallization quality.
