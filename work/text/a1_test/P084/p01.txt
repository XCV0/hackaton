High-Resistance Metal Oxide Window Layers for Optimal
Front Contact Interfaces in Sb2Se3 Solar Cells
Joao O. Mendes, Enrico Della Gaspera, and Joel van Embden*
1. Introduction
Antimony chalcogenides (Sb2Se3,Sb2S3,Sb2SxSe3/C0x) have come
to light as excellent materials for optoelectronic applications.
Remarkable device performances have been demonstrated for
solar cells, photoelectrochemical cells, and photodetectors.[1]
Speciﬁc attention has been given to thin-ﬁlm solar cells with a
Sb2Se3 light-absorbing layer, which have experienced rapid evo-
lution in power conversion efﬁciency (PCE).[2] The characteristics
that make Sb2Se3 an outstanding light-absorbing material for
solar cells include an attractive bandgap of 1.1–1.2 eV, high
optical absorption coefﬁcient (>10/C05 cm/C01),
and a decent electron mobility
(>17 cm2 V/C01 s/C01 along the c-axis).[3]
Sb2Se3 adopts a 1D orthorhombic crystal
structure (Pbnm space group) that naturally
results in thin ﬁlms with self-passivating
grain boundaries.[4] Finally, Sb2Se3 forms
only one crystal phase (orthorhombic)
and has a low vapor pressure, making this
material ideal for the facile low-cost
processing of high-quality thin ﬁlms by
evaporative processes.[5]
Evaporated Sb2Se3 solar cells on molyb-
denum substrates with nanostructured and
highly (hk1) oriented grains (c-axis perpen-
dicular to the substrate) have achieved a
record 9.2% PCE,[6] while planar super-
strate devices have reached a lower PCE
of 7.6%.[7] At present, the major challenges
facing Sb2Se3 superstrate devices are both
the poor interfaces among absorber, buffer,
and window layers at the front contact, as
well as the need to direct crystal orientation
of the (Sb4Se6)n 1D ribbons in the Sb2Se3
ﬁlm to achieve optimal performance.[8]
To address the ﬁrst of these challenges various buffer materials
have been tested as n-type front contacts wherein CdS, TiO2, and
ZnO are the most common. Notably, regardless of device archi-
tecture or processing method, a CdS buffer is ubiquitous in top-
performing Sb2Se3 solar cells. CdS has a compatible conduction
band position with Sb2Se3 (/C25–4 eV relative to vacuum), a high-
electron mobility (350 cm2 V/C01 s/C01) and is still the most preferred
layer for achieving highly efﬁcient Sb2Se3 devices.[8a,9] A thin
CdS layer is desirable to minimize parasitic absorption of
short-wavelength light. However, reducing its thickness to less
than /C2550–60 nm is not practical as this inevitably results in ﬁlm
discontinuities and a higher density of pinholes that yield devices
with low shunt resistance.[10] This is further compounded by the
fact that CdS can be consumed by ion diffusion at the CdS/
Sb2Se3 interface.[9a,11] A common strategy to address this has
been to apply a high resistance metal oxide (HRMO) layer at
the front contact prior to CdS buffer deposition to provide high
shunt protection. In this approach, a layer consisting of a ﬁlm of
an HRMO with a wide bandgap is deposited between the trans-
parent conductive oxide (TCO) and buffer layers.
In CdTe and Cu(In,Ga)Se2 solar cells interfacial layers have
been shown to affect device performance,[12] and the beneﬁts of
applying an HRMO interfacial layer in chalcogenide solar cells
with a CdS buffer are well established.[13] However, not all
metal oxides are adequate as high resistance layers e.g., while

Herein, an in-depth experimental investigation into the effect of employing
different high resistance metal oxide (HRMO) layers on the quality of the front
contact in solar cells with an ﬂuorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/(HRMO)/CdS/
Sb2Se3/Au device architecture is presented. The application of ZnO or TiO2
HRMO layers between FTO substrates and CdS improves the overall device
performance. Short-circuit current gains of /C2520%, orders of magnitude higher
shunt resistances (/C25104 Ω cm2), and greatly improved device stabilities—
maintaining over 95% of their initial efﬁciency over 137 days are observed. A
suppression of the unfavorable (120) orientation of the photoactive Sb2Se3
layer is observed in devices with HRMO interlayers. The application of HRMO
layers is crucial to prevent both ohmic and non-ohmic current leaks and
maintain device stability over time. Cross-over in the current-voltage ( JV )
curves observed in the case of TiO2 indicates the presence of a high barrier for
the diode current in these devices. Wavelength-dependent JV curves coupled
with capacitance measurements and simulations show that this barrier can be
attributed to a high density of interfacial acceptor states. In contrast, ZnO
deposition is found to reduce interface defects and enhance the quality of the
front contact, while boosting performance and increasing device longevity.
