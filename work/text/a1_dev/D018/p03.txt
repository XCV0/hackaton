A high-temperature cracker stage (/C24600/C14C) breaks large Se molecules
into small ones, which increases the reactivity of the Se species on
the deposition surface.
A two-stage deposition process3 was used to obtain a favorable
Ga grading in the absorber. In the first stage, a stoichiometric target
(Cu0.50In0.35Ga0.15, Testbourne Ltd.) was used while evaporating Se in
pulses with 1 s period and 100 ms duty cycle. During /C2480 min.
deposition time, a slightly Cu-rich CIGS layer of around /C241,500 nm
thickness formed. In the second stage, a /C24 600 nm thick In-Se layer
was deposited by sputtering from an indium target and simultaneously
evaporating Se in pulses with 1 s period and 300 ms duty cycle in the
same chamber without breaking the vacuum. This two-stage process
effectively eliminates the unwanted Cu2-xSe impurity phase and the
need for KCN etching before the chemical bath deposition (CBD) of
the buffer layer.
A CsCO3 (purity 99.99%, Sigma Aldrich) solution was prepared by
dissolving 0.01 g of CsCO3 in 10 ml of deionized water. The as-grown
CIGS thin films were then spin-coated with the solution at 1000 rpm
for 60 s. Cs-ST and untreated CIGS thin films were transferred into
the PHRMS chamber and annealed at 400/C14C for 2 minutes at the
chamber pressure of /C24 6 /C2 10/C06 mbar. No additional Se was supplied
during the annealing. When the substrate temperature decreased to
around 50/C14C, the samples were taken out from the vacuum system
and proceeded to the buffer layer deposition.
Before deposition of the cadmium sulfide (CdS) buffer layer, the
samples (Cs-ST and untreated CIGS thin films) were rinsed separately
with de-ionized (DI) water. The CdS buffer layer was prepared with a
solution of 1.33 g of thiourea (SC [NH2]2), 0.118 g of cadmium acetate
in 15 ml of NH3, and 80 ml of DI water at 60/C14C. The CdS deposition
was performed in a separate solution for Cs-ST and untreated CIGS
thin films to avoid any cross-contamination.
Solar cells were completed by deposition of /C2450 nm intrinsic-
ZnO (i-ZnO) and /C24200 nm aluminum-doped ZnO (ZnO:Al) layers by
radio-frequency sputtering at room temperature in the window
chamber of the STAR system at 5.6 /C2 10/C03 mbar Ar pressure and
with 40 and 60 W, respectively.
The CIGS morphology was determined by scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) in an FEI Quanta 650 FEG SEM microscope
equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) for
compositional analysis (using 30 keV acceleration voltage). The JV
characteristics of solar cell devices were measured in a solar simulator
(Oriel Sol3A class AAA) under AM1.5 illumination. Devices were
manually scribed to /C240.25 cm2 and measured without a front grid.
UPS and XPS were performed in an ESCALAB-150Xi (Thermo
Fisher Scientific). UPS was performed using He I (21.2 eV) radiation.
An Ar gas cluster ion beam of 1,000 Ar atoms and energy 4,000 eV
generated by a MAGCIS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) ion source was
used to remove surface contaminations without damaging the surface.
Depth profiling was realized by a monoatomic Ar+ ion beam with
1 keV kinetic energy and 3 μA current and intermittent XPS analysis
every 30 s for the first five cycles and every 90 s onward.
The preparation of the samples for the transmission electron
microscope (HRTEM) analysis was carried out by a focused ion beam
(Dual Beam FIB-SEM Helios 450S). A double-corrected TEM/STEM
Titan Themis operating at 200 kV was used to take high-resolution
high-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron
microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images, and energy dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDX) maps.
Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) was performed with a
Bruker Dimension Icon atomic force microscope (AFM) operated in
ambient conditions. We used amplitude-modulated KPFM in the dual
pass with a lift height of 10 nm. Pt/Ir coated cantilevers (PPP-EFM
Nanosensors) with a nominal tip radius of 25 nm, spring constant of
2.8 N/m, and 75 kHz resonance frequency were employed for the
measurements and calibrated using an Au-coated Si sample to ensure
comparability of the results. The contact potential difference (CPD) is
defined as VCPD = /C0VDC = e(Φsample /C0 Φtip), where Φ is the work
function. According to the set-up a higher CPD means a higher
work function of the sample surface.
3 | RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
SEM images of Cs-ST and untreated CIGS thin films after rinsing with
DI water (Figure 2) show no significant changes in the surface
morphology upon the Cs-ST, indicating that CsCO3 did not form a
visible inseparable layer as observed for the CsF-PDT of CIGS in
vacuum.28–30 Nevertheless, XPS spectra of the CIGS thin films with
(red curve) and without (black curve) Cs-ST (Figure 2C) clearly show
two additional peaks at binding energies of 723 and 737 eV for the
Cs-ST sample, corresponding to the Cs 3d5/2 and Cs 3d3/2 lines,
respectively, which confirms the presence of Cs at the surface of the
Cs-ST CIGS thin films.
To get further insights into the change of the surface properties,
detailed XPS spectra of the Cu 2p3/2,In3d5/2,Ga2p3/2, Se 3d, and Cs
3d5/2 peaks were measured (Figure 3). These spectra reveal that Cu is
depleted at the near-surface region in the Cs-ST CIGS thin film (solid
lines) as compared to the untreated CIGS film (dotted lines). After
sputter-etching the CIGS thin film to a depth of around 40 nm, the Cu
peak intensity of the Cs-ST sample coincides with that of the
untreated CIGS thin film, suggesting the formation of a Cu-deficient
layer of around /C2440 nm at the surface of the Cs-ST sample. Similarly,
the peak intensity of In 3d5/2 (Figure 3B), Ga 2p3/2 (Figure 3C), and Se
3d (Figure 3D) decreased at the very near surface region (0 nm) and
increased when going deeper (> 20 nm). In our sample, even after DI
water rinsing, the intensity of the Ga 2p3/2 peak did not reduce, indi-
cating that the change of surface properties in Cs-ST CIGS is different
from that of AlkF-PDT CIGS.11 The peak intensity of Cs 3d5/2
(Figure 3E), on the other hand, increased at the surface region, indicat-
ing the formation of a Cs-related layer at the surface.
