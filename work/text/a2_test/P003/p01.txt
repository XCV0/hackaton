Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can halluci-
nate in specialized medical QA and remain
poorly calibrated. We propose Uncertainty-
guided Retrieval-augmented Generation (UR-
Gen), a training-free framework for disease-
scoped, guideline-grounded multiple-choice
QA. URGen ranks guideline keypoints un-
der multiple temperatures, uses ranking en-
tropy to select retrieval depth, aggregates
multi-temperature answers, and fuses nor-
malized context and answer entropy. On
TED with Llama-3.1-8B, ﬁve-seed evaluation
gives 80.40 ± 1.29% accuracy, 1.98 ± 0.00%
overconﬁdent errors, and 6.80 ± 1.13% ex-
pected calibration error. Paired bootstrap
tests show lower overconﬁdent error than ev-
ery ﬁxed depth (p ≤ 0.0016), while accu-
racy remains comparable to Fixed-1 (p =
0.0630). A broader descriptive compari-
son covers two thyroid-related ophthalmology
benchmarks and three backbones. The ev-
idence therefore supports URGen primarily
as a reliability-oriented method for controlled
guideline galleries, rather than a general web-
scale retriever.
1 Introduction
Trustworthy medical question answering (QA) can
reduce misdiagnosis, promote consistent interpre-
tation of clinical guidelines, and broaden access
to specialist knowledge (Jin et al., 2021; Pal et al.,
2022; Wang et al., 2025; Rezaei et al., 2025). How-
ever, medical questions often depend on long-tail,
domain-speciﬁc facts that lie outside the pretrain-
ing distribution of general-purpose LLMs (Vilares
and Gómez-Rodríguez, 2019; Xu et al., 2025). As
a result, even strong models usually produce ﬂu-
ent but incorrect answers with high conﬁdence (Li
et al., 2023; Simhi et al., 2025), which is unaccept-
able in safety-critical settings.

In Figure 1, we illustrate the failure mode on
an ophthalmology guideline question, where both
Llama-3.1-70B (Llama Team, 2024) and GPT-4o-
2024-1120 (OpenAI, 2023) return hallucinated an-
swers without meaningful semantic-level uncer-
tainty signals (despite token-based or p(True)-
style conﬁdence estimates (Kadavath et al., 2022;
Tian et al., 2023)). Therefore, reliable medical
QA requires (i) grounding answers in authorita-
tive external evidence and (ii) calibrated uncer-
tainty estimates that reﬂect ambiguity, missing ev-
idence, or conﬂicts (Guo et al., 2017; Kuleshov
et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2024). This improves
transparency and overall user-friendliness, foster-
ing greater trust and willingness to use the AI
among patients and clinicians.
