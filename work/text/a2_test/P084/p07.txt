Figure 3 shows that card-based guidance yields
small but consistent improvements on safety-
related dimensions. The relatively larger move-
ments (on the order of +0.1–0.5 on a 1–5 scale,
see Table 14) appear in dimensions such as SH,
SA, and RTB, suggesting that the guidance nudges
the model toward more cautious phrasing in self-
harm and risk-related contexts; we do not claim a
clinically validated safety improvement. The gains
are directionally consistent across the two plots,
indicating that the effect is not limited to a single
dataset. We note that baseline safety scores are
already in the 3.7–4.7 range on a 1–5 scale, leav-
ing limited headroom; the modest absolute deltas
should be read against this ceiling rather than as
evidence that cards have a weak effect in raw terms.
Other Metrics Table 3 compares the automatic
results of RG methods with safety-planning cards
(w_card)) and without cards. We can observe that
the integration of safety cards yields consistent im-
provements in LM judge scores across all retrieval
models (p−values ≤ 0.05 with 95% CI using
pair-wise t−test). However, for other metrics, al-
though BLEU scores generally show improvement,
ROUGE and BERTScore exhibit slight declines or
remain relatively stagnant when safety cards are
used. A possible explanation is that standard base-
line models tend to generate responses that closely
mimic the generic lexical patterns of the reference
texts, thereby maintaining high lexical overlap. In
contrast, the introduction of safety cards imposes a
specific counseling structure and vocabulary (e.g.,
explicit validation steps). It may reduce lexical
overlap that limits ROUGE and BERTScore.
5.2.2 Human Evaluation
Five independent evaluators reviewed 100 ran-
dom question-response pairs from different models
based on the previously defined Counseling Qual-
ity and Safety Quality metrics (Section 5.2.1). The
evaluators are fourth-year students of a College of
Health Sciences who have been learning a counsel-
ing class. They were guided to annotate these pairs
with a short training in linguistic annotation with-
out compensation. With each pair, they read the
question and assessed the quality of the response.
Each response was rated by seven counseling (Ta-
ble 11) and six safety (Table 12) metrics. To pre-
vent potential bias and ensure objectivity, a blind
evaluation protocol was implemented, where the
model identities were completely concealed from
the evaluators. The Fleiss’ kappa score is 0.740 for
MentalChat and 0.710 for CounselChat.
